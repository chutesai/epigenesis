\begin{abstract}
We call a model \emph{epigenetic} if what it learns after deployment never edits its base weights: acquired
knowledge lives in a separate sparse layer that can be removed, restoring the original model exactly.
A \texttt{pair8}-quantized network holds at least half of its weights at structural zero. We study whether
those zeros can serve as a continual-learning \emph{reserve}: freeze the learned weights, write new
knowledge only into the zero slots, and recover the old behavior with a per-slice mask. This is exactly
\citet{packnet}, placed in a format whose zeros are already addressable. On a synthetic knowledge-capacity
assay we quantify the \emph{mechanism} (dense fp32, random mask): the masked base is preserved exactly (by
construction), the reserve memorizes random facts up to a measured maximum and then declines, a
mask-free ``soft'' variant degrades rather than stays flat, and---counted in the representation we actually
train---tuned LoRA is \emph{both more capable and more storage-efficient}. The contribution is therefore a
careful, honest characterization of the mechanism and its accounting, plus a scoped proposal: a
\emph{revocable domain patch} inside a quantized model, whose natural application---a private, continually
adapting model---we motivate but do not claim to have built. We state precisely what is untested and the
single experiment (ternary reserve through the real quantizer) that would resolve whether a storage
advantage exists---an outcome we argue is genuinely open, not forecastable from the format assay.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Adding knowledge to a trained model usually means adding parameters---a LoRA adapter \citep{lora}, an extra
expert, or a full fine-tune that risks forgetting. We ask a narrower question: an extreme-quantized model
has already paid for a pool of weight slots held at zero; can those zeros double as a place to put new
knowledge?

\textbf{Free-Slot Adaptation (FSA).} Store a model in format $\Phi$ (here \texttt{pair8}: ternary weights,
at most two of every four aligned pairs non-zero). Let $\Sz$ be the non-zero support and $\Zz$ the zero
reserve. To learn a new domain $d$: freeze $\Sz$ and shared parameters, train a disjoint slice
$\Rd \subseteq \Zz$, and store a mask $M_d$. At inference the base uses $\Sz$; domain $d$ uses
$\Sz \cup \Rd$. The mechanism is \citet{packnet}'s; the only new element is that the free weights come from
the compression format rather than a pruning pass.

\textbf{What we claim, and what we do not.} We claim a clean characterization of this mechanism on a
synthetic assay: it preserves the masked base exactly, it memorizes a bounded amount of new material, and
its cost is a \emph{trade against compression}, not free capacity. We do \emph{not} claim it beats
parameter-efficient adaptation---our own tuned-LoRA results show the opposite---nor that it is validated on
a real model or in the ternary format it is motivated by. We are explicit about this throughout, because an
earlier draft overstated it and adversarial review corrected us.

\textbf{Contributions.} (1) The framing of quantization zeros as a continual-learning reserve, with honest
accounting of what is free (logical parameter count, dense-kernel FLOPs) and what is not (storage, which
trades against compression; the mask; the scales). (2) A synthetic assay isolating the mechanism: exact
masked retention, a measured reserve ceiling and interference regime, a mask-free soft variant, and a
indicative (uncontrolled) LoRA comparison that we report even though it is unfavorable. (3) A scoped proposal---a revocable
domain patch inside a quantized model---and an honest discussion of where it could matter (a private,
continually-learning model) and where retrieval already wins. (4) The gating experiment (ternary reserve
through the real quantizer) whose outcome is open.

\section{Background and related work}
\textbf{Mask-based continual learning.} PackNet \citep{packnet} freezes kept weights and trains freed ones,
recovering a task by its mask; Piggyback \citep{piggyback} \emph{learns masks over fixed weights} (it does
not refill zeros); HAT \citep{hat} uses hard task-attention and reports \emph{reduced}, not zero,
forgetting; Supermasks \citep{supermasks} make the mask itself the trainable object; Progressive Networks
\citep{progressivenn} add and could drop columns. FSA is PackNet with the free support supplied by the
quantization format. \textbf{Quantized continual learning.} Ada-QPackNet \citep{adaqpacknet} and
TinySubNets \citep{tinysubnets} already combine PackNet-style capacity reuse with quantization; SPDF
\citep{spdf} reopens zeroed pretrained weights for dense fine-tuning. These sharply narrow our novelty; our
distinct element is a reserve \emph{pre-addressed by the deployed format} and composed by union.
\textbf{Parameter-efficient and continual adapters.} LoRA \citep{lora}, QLoRA \citep{qlora}, QA-LoRA
\citep{qalora}; \citet{loralearnsless} find LoRA learns and forgets less than full fine-tuning (a possible
rank explanation, not a theorem); O-LoRA \citep{olora} does continual learning in orthogonal LoRA
subspaces. \textbf{Sparse / composable adaptation.} Diff pruning \citep{diffpruning} and composable sparse
fine-tuning \citep{composablesft}; union of \emph{disjoint} sparse supports is the easy case of the latter.
\textbf{Editing, memory, unlearning.} MEMIT \citep{memit} edits facts in place and can override; FSA adds paths that can
suppress or correct outputs while enabled but cannot edit a base weight; GRACE \citep{grace} and WISE \citep{wise} keep deletable, routed side-memory for sequential edits
while freezing the base; SISA \citep{sisa} is exact unlearning by shard isolation; TOFU \citep{tofu}
benchmarks fictitious-fact forgetting. \textbf{Personalization and retrieval.} OPPU \citep{oppu} attaches a
per-user PEFT module (removed by user identity, not per record); LaMP \citep{lamp} is a personalization
benchmark with retrieval baselines; RAG \citep{rag} and kNN-LM \citep{knnlm} store facts externally and
delete them by dropping a record---the baseline any ``delete a fact'' claim must beat.
\textbf{Low-bit models.} BitNet b1.58 \citep{bitnet158,bitnet2b4t} and LittleBit \citep{littlebit}; our
assay methodology follows knowledge-capacity scaling \citep{knowcap}.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Format and reserve}
In \texttt{pair8}, weights form aligned blocks of eight (four pairs); each weight is ternary times a scale,
and at most two pairs per block are non-zero. A block thus has $417$ reachable states
($1 + 4\!\cdot\!8 + \binom{4}{2}8^2$), i.e. $\log_2 417/8 \approx 1.088$ bits/weight asymptotically
($1.125$ at a fixed $9$ bits/block), and is $\ge 50\%$ zero \emph{by construction} (ternary alone
guarantees no such fraction). \textbf{Caveat on the reserve size.} At a density that already uses both
allowed pairs in a block, that block has \emph{no in-codebook room} for a third pair. So the writable
reserve is not the full $(1-\rho)N$; either the base must leave pairs unused, or a patch must leave the
\texttt{pair8} codebook and live in a \emph{sidecar} of \texttt{(block, pair, trit, scale)} entries that the
kernel applies or skips. A pre-assigned ternary reserve pair costs $\approx \log_2 3 = 1.58$ bits/weight,
not $1.088$ (that figure is the joint entropy spread over the forced zeros, not the marginal price of a
filled slot).

\subsection{Adaptation and the soft variant}
\textbf{Hard FSA, domain $d$:} freeze $W[\Sz]$, all shared parameters, and---crucially for the real
format---the discrete pair occupancy and the per-row scales; train only $W[\Rd]$ ($\Rd \cap \Sz = \varnothing$)
with the base live; store $M_d$. Inference: base $\to \Sz$; domain $\to \Sz\cup\Rd$ (needs a task ID).
\textbf{Soft FSA} removes the task ID: freeze the base, train the reserve with a KL penalty keeping
base-input outputs unchanged, and run unmasked. This is Learning-without-Forgetting \citep{lwf} on a frozen
disjoint support, with full base-input replay---a substantial rehearsal resource we note explicitly.

\subsection{Honest accounting}
FSA adds \textbf{zero new logical parameters} (same element count) and \textbf{zero extra FLOPs on a dense
GEMM}---but the latter is kernel-conditional: a pair-sparse kernel (the kind the format exists to enable)
does \emph{more} work once zeros are filled. It is \textbf{not free in storage}: the deployable form is a
frozen base pack plus a sidecar (new stored state) whose cost is the trits written ($\approx 1.58$
bits/weight), their addresses, and any scale overrides, plus the mask ($1$ bit/weight/domain, or
$\approx 0$ only with a fixed structural/hash rule---a $1$-bit mask over $100$B weights is $12.5$\,GB).
At densities that already use the two allowed pairs in a block, the codebook is full, so a filled patch is \emph{added bytes} on a packed base (a sidecar), not a reallocation of the base's own compression budget. Freezing the shared scales is also a
\emph{capacity cut}: a reserve trit can only take values $\{-\alpha, 0, +\alpha\}$ for the base row's
per-row absmean scale $\alpha$.

\section{Theory}
\textbf{Base invariance (by construction, narrow).} Restrict the forward to $M_0$. If adaptation changes
only $W[\Rd]$ with $\Rd\cap\Sz=\varnothing$ and freezes all shared state, the masked base output is
unchanged---an invariance of freezing, which is why measured ``retention $=1.000$'' is a unit test that the
mask was applied (it would hold even if the reserve learned nothing). \emph{Three subtleties:} (i) it is the
\emph{masked} base; with the reserve enabled, the base behavior is not preserved (we measure strongly
negative unmasked retention). (ii) In the real \texttt{pair8} format the decoded weight is
$\alpha\cdot\text{trit}$ and the quantizer recomputes the pair occupancy and scale each forward; invariance
then requires freezing \emph{three} objects---the masters, the occupancy, and the scales---not just the
masters. (iii) The soft variant is outside this argument entirely.

\textbf{Reserve capacity is accounting, not a law.} We do not vary $\rho$ or the per-slot cost, so the curve
is a memorization curve, not a test of a capacity expression. We therefore report
$K_{\text{reserve}} \approx \kappa_{\text{adapt}}\,B_{\text{patch}}$ with $B_{\text{patch}}$ the measured patch
\emph{bits} and $\kappa_{\text{adapt}}$ an \emph{observed} knowledge-bits-per-stored-bit efficiency
conditional on base, support, scales, and training budget---and note it has no term for the interference
decline we plot.

\section{Experiments}
Synthetic knowledge-capacity assay: disjoint random fact sets $A\to B$ (key$\to$label, $V{=}1024$, so each
fact is $10$ bits of arbitrary lookup), $K = N\log_2 V - \sum\text{CE}/\ln 2$. A $917$k-parameter MLP
($\rho{=}0.5$) is loaded with 128k base facts (base recall only $0.744$---``saturated'' is relative), then
adapted. \textbf{The reserve is a dense fp32 mask}: this is why everything here is a mechanism
demonstration. Single seed; LoRA uses a fixed scale $2.0$ (not the standard $\alpha/r$ of \citet{lora}) with
its LR tuned down per load to compensate; it also uses AdamW weight decay $0.01$ (vs FSA's $0$) and sees a
dense base (recall $\approx\!0.64$) while FSA sees a $50\%$-masked base (recall $0.744$)---so the comparison
is indicative, not controlled.

\section{A proposed direction (untested): a revocable patch, and a personal model}
\label{sec:direction}
What survives review is narrow: if the ternary mechanism works (Gate~0), a quantized model could carry a
\textbf{revocable domain patch}---task ID in, slice on; drop the slice and the \emph{masked} base returns.
A precise statement avoids the overclaims of our earlier draft:

\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Patch-revocation contract.} Deleting an isolated patch exactly removes \emph{that patch's parameter
contribution}. Equivalence to a model trained without its data additionally requires that no retained patch
or training state depended on it. It does \emph{not} delete individual facts (facts within a slice are
superimposed), and it does not make information already in the base inaccessible.
\end{quote}

This is weaker than, and should be compared against, deleting a row from a retrieval store
\citep{rag,knnlm}---which is per-record and leaves the base bit-identical---or disabling a per-user adapter
\citep{oppu}, which removes that user's whole contribution without a weight-unlearning problem. The honest case for putting anything in the \emph{weights} is for
skills, style, or policy that benefit from parametric adaptation and must act in every forward pass---not
for arbitrary facts, which are retrieval's job and which our own tuned-LoRA numbers memorize more cheaply.
A ``living personal model'' that learns continually and on-device is an appealing target for these
properties, but two things remain unestablished and we do not claim them: that personal \emph{skills, style, or
policy} (not arbitrary facts, which our assay shows tuned LoRA memorizes more cheaply) benefit from
parametric adaptation over retrieval, and that \emph{training} (not inference) fits
on-device---the reserve fill is a half-tensor, so AdamW state alone is order $10$\,GB at a few billion
weights. The right evaluation pairs FSA against OPPU-style per-user adapters and local retrieval on a real
personalization benchmark \citep{lamp}, with unlearning measured \citep{sisa,tofu}.

\section{Limitations and the deciding experiment}
\label{sec:limits}
Every number here is a \textbf{dense fp32 mechanism demonstration}; the format and real models are untested.
\textbf{Gate~0:} rerun the assay through the real quantizer---\emph{ternary patches on a \texttt{pair8}
base}, pair occupancy and scales frozen, reserve trained as trits with straight-through estimation (note
this is sidecar-style, since an in-codebook block at two pairs cannot open a third). Publish $K$ vs fact
count beside the fp32 curve and sidecar bytes beside FP16 and ternary LoRA at matched budget. The
outcome is \emph{genuinely open}: the \texttt{pair8} format assay measures \emph{from-scratch} QAT at matched
loads (its saturation sweep unfinished), so its $\kappa$ cannot predict a \emph{frozen-base} reserve---and
even a pessimistic ternary yield, priced at the corrected $\approx\!1.58$ bits/trit, need not fall below fp32
LoRA's $0.057$. Gate~0, not the assay, decides. Further: $K$ is a log-loss improvement, not semantic
knowledge; $\kappa$ is assay- and load-specific (at matched load \texttt{pair8}'s lower bit-rate gives a
\emph{higher} $\kappa$ than plain ternary; ternary only reaches more \emph{total} knowledge at a higher
budget); the frozen base can augment or suppress through new paths but cannot refine.

\section{Conclusion}
Freezing a disjoint mask preserves the masked function; a quantization reserve memorizes random facts up to
a cliff \emph{in the dense fp32 mechanism}; a KL penalty buys a degrading always-on curve. Stated
carefully---and only for that fp32 mask---the mechanism is small and true; the ternary reserve that would
make it matter is untested. Whether it becomes a \emph{useful} revocable patch---and the substrate for a
private model that learns with one person and can \emph{revoke a patch} on request---depends first on the
ternary reserve carrying real knowledge at a competitive byte cost, which Gate~0 would decide. That
experiment, not another
retention-$1.000$ plot, is the next step.
